Dark Green Canopy & Berry Set Coffee plants have been observed to develop a noticeably dark-green appearance during the period of regular bioslurry use. During the recent coffee blossom period and shortly afterwards, the estate applied the fermented bioslurry as a foliar spray twice within the month. These observations are encouraging, but the website records them as an association rather than proof of causation. A working hypothesis is that improved nutrient availability, plant vigour and microbial nutrient mobilisation/solubilisation may have contributed to the response. The estate will continue to compare flowering, fruit set, plant colour and yield with application records. Bioslurry Fermentation — Practical Field Indicators Fermentation is assessed on the estate after approximately 5–7 days, using a combination of fermentation time, physical appearance, consistency, colour and odour rather than appearance alone. • Good fermentation: the fermented bioslurry develops a smooth, diluted consistency resembling boiled rice water. • Odour: a characteristic fermented smell may develop, described from field observation as a mixture of alcohol-like and mild sulfurous notes. • Field judgement: consistency, odour and the fermentation period are used together as practical indicators that a batch is ready. • Black batches: if a fermented solution turns black, it is not retained for further multiplication. The batch may still be used for field fertigation. These are practical on-farm indicators of fermentation condition. Colour, smell and consistency do not by themselves quantify microbial populations or confirm the concentration of individual organisms; laboratory viable counts would be required for that purpose. Mother Culture Preparation & 10-Barrel Multiplication The estate prepares and multiplies its on-farm microbial consortium in a simple rolling system before field fertigation through the borewell and Winkler network. Mother Culture — 225 L 2 kg talc-based bioslurry formulation + 2–3 packets OWDC + 6–10 kg fresh cow dung + 1 kg gram flour (besan) + 1 kg jaggery. Make up to 225 L and allow to ferment for approximately 5–7 days until ready. Divide Into 10 Barrels Once the mother culture is ready, it is divided into 10 × 225-L barrels. Each barrel retains approximately 10–15% culture for continuation, receives 1 kg jaggery + 1 kg gram flour, and is made up to 225 L. Rolling Multiplication Each barrel is stirred 2–3 times per day for 1–2 minutes. After approximately 6–8 days, around 85–90% is used for field fertigation while the retained portion continues the next cycle. Fresh Mother Culture A fresh mother culture is prepared after every four multiplication cycles. Soil & Microbes The estate's biological-management system combines a purchased microbial consortium, on-farm multiplication, bioslurry fertigation, foliar/soil applications, OWDC-assisted biomass decomposition and progressive reduction of synthetic NPK. Microbial Consortium Supplied by the Laboratory The purchased formulation was developed after detailed discussion with the formulation scientist/laboratory. The supplied consortium is then fermented or multiplied on the farm. The formulation includes fungal bioagents, beneficial bacteria, entomopathogenic fungi, nutrient-solubilizing microorganisms, nitrogen-fixing bacteria and micronutrient-associated components. Proprietary formulation note: The laboratory has deliberately not disclosed some microorganisms associated with the mobilisation of Fe, Zn and Si because those details are treated as trade-secret/proprietary information. The public website therefore does not attempt to identify those undisclosed organisms. How We Judge Fermentation Readiness After approximately 5–7 days of fermentation, the estate also uses practical physical observations before deciding how the batch will be used. A ready batch is observed to develop a consistency similar to boiled rice water, together with a characteristic alcoholic/fermented smell and a sulphur-like smell. These are recorded as on-farm observations, not as laboratory tests of microbial viability. Colour is also observed. If the bioslurry turns black, the batch is not used for further multiplication, but, according to the estate's current field practice, it can still be used for fertigation. Mother Culture Preparation The estate prepares a fresh mother culture that is then used to initiate subsequent bioslurry multiplication cycles. The current on-farm preparation uses 2 kg of the talc-based bioslurry formulation, 2–3 packets of OWDC, 6–10 kg fresh cow dung, 1 kg gram flour (besan) and 1 kg jaggery. The mixture is allowed to ferment for approximately 5–7 days until considered ready for use as mother culture. 2 kg talc-based bioslurry + 2–3 OWDC packets + 6–10 kg fresh cow dung + 1 kg gram flour + 1 kg jaggery → 5–7 days This is the estate's current mother-culture preparation practice, based on the formulation developed with the laboratory/scientist. It is a farm protocol, not a universal application recommendation. On-Farm Bioslurry Multiplication Protocol The estate uses 10 barrels of 225-L working volume. After the mother culture is ready, the 225-L mother culture is divided among 10 multiplication barrels, and each barrel is made up to approximately 225 L. About 10–15% is retained from each barrel as inoculum for the next cycle. A fresh mother culture is prepared after every four multiplication cycles. Retain 10–15% → + 1 kg jaggery + + 1 kg gram flour → Make up to 225 L → Stir 2–3×/day → ~5–7 days Contents are stirred approximately 2–3 times per day for 1–2 minutes. The current working cycle is approximately 5–7 days, with readiness judged mainly from physical appearance and smell. This is the estate's current farm protocol, not a universal application recommendation. Microbial-growth hypothesis: The estate expects nutrient-cycling organisms and Trichoderma to multiply relatively readily under favourable fermentation conditions, while entomopathogenic fungi may establish more slowly. However, relative microbial concentration is not assumed from appearance alone; confirming changes in CFU or viable counts would require laboratory analysis. Successive multiplication cycles may therefore alter the microbial balance, which is an area for future measurement. Whole-Farm Bioslurry Fertigation The bioslurry is not manually poured around plants. Because manual application of multiple barrels is not economical, the farm continuously feeds bioslurry into the main borewell delivery/deadline pipe using an HPT sprayer pump while the Winkler irrigation network is running. ~100–110 L Bioslurry per ~15-minute fertigation shift 5–6 Winklers Approx. area group per fertigation shift ~50 Winklers/acre Approx. estate Winkler density ~6.1–6.5 kL Approx. bioslurry applied over a full 7.5-acre round During fertigation, the farm changes the irrigated group approximately every 15 minutes and continues until the working barrels are exhausted. A typical multiplication cycle provides 9 working barrels × 225–240 L ≈ 2,025–2,160 L; approximately three such 9-barrel outputs are currently used to cover the entire 7.5-acre estate. Important distinction: normal irrigation and fertigation have different operating schedules. Normal irrigation runs about 1–1 h 10 min per 5–7 Winklers. The fertigation operation uses repeated 15-minute bioslurry-injection shifts as the farm moves from one area to the next. Current Synthetic NPK Baseline During the current nutrient-management period, the estate has continued to use reduced synthetic fertilizer inputs alongside the biological program. One reported coffee application included 10 bags of DAP, 50 kg each (18-46-0), 6 bags of urea, 45 kg each, and 5 bags of MOP, 50 kg each (0-0-60). For this application, the product quantities were approximately 500 kg DAP (18-46-0), 270 kg urea, and 250 kg MOP (0-0-60). Using the stated fertilizer grades, this corresponds to approximately 139.5 kg total N, 230 kg P2O5, and 150 kg K2O. Older pepper vines also received approximately 50–75 g per vine of additional fertilizer around catkin initiation; the exact product grade for that pepper application is kept as a separate field record. The intention is not to stop mineral nutrition abruptly, but to use soil and crop response to decide how far synthetic NPK can be progressively tapered while bioslurry, organic biomass recycling and microbial nutrient cycling continue. Annual Bioslurry Fertigation Plan The current plan is to maintain several whole-estate bioslurry fertigation rounds rather than treat one application as a complete nutrient replacement. Two rounds were completed in February and March 2026, further rounds are being carried out in August and September, and an additional October round may be added depending on field conditions. This is being treated as a practical annual management baseline while the estate continues to observe soil condition, plant growth, flowering, fruit set and yield before making further reductions in synthetic NPK. Biomass Recycling + OWDC Brush cutting generates substantial green mulch about every 40–50 days. The estate also produces green litter from coffee pruning, Halwanna, Silver Oak and forest-tree pruning. This biomass is being returned to the system, with OWDC used together with bioslurry to support decomposition and organic-matter recycling. The wider research question is whether this continuous return of biomass, combined with microbial nutrient mobilisation and bioslurry fertigation, can gradually reduce dependence on externally supplied synthetic NPK while maintaining crop performance. Soil Structure & Green Biomass The estate is also observing a gradual change in soil structure as green litter from brush cutting and pruning is repeatedly returned to the soil surface. Coffee, shade-tree and other estate biomass is being retained as mulch rather than removed. Together with bioslurry and OWDC-assisted decomposition, this is being investigated as a possible contributor to improving organic-matter cycling and soil physical condition. The present observation is encouraging, but the website deliberately does not claim that bioslurry or mulch alone caused the change. Future soil-organic-matter, aggregation, moisture-retention and biological measurements can strengthen the record. Research & Field Experiments SAARTHA ESTATE treats field management as an ongoing set of observations and experiments. Claims are separated into observed results, working hypotheses and future plans. Arabica Leaf Rust Current observation: no significant visible leaf-rust activity observed in the monitored Arabica areas during the current observation period despite no synthetic fungicide applications. Working hypothesis: the combined biological environment—including Lecanicillium lecanii, Trichoderma viride/T. harzianum, plant vigour, shade, soil biology and weather—may contribute to lower disease pressure. No single organism is claimed to be solely responsible. White Coffee Stem Borer (WCSB) WCSB remains an active research area. The current approach combines shade management, biological inputs, natural predators, monitoring and a planned botanical/mineral surface-treatment program. Current spray formulation: 1–2% lime + 0.5% neem oil + 0.1% DDL gum. The estate is not aiming for a thick lime coating at this concentration; the working hypothesis is that the treated stem surface may be less favourable for adult landing and oviposition, while neem may provide additional repellency. Schedule: four preventive spray rounds each year — September, October, March and April — covering the two main observation windows of September–October and March–April. Another working hypothesis is that entomopathogenic fungi already present on treated stems may interact with eggs or early stages. Beauveria bassiana and Metarhizium anisopliae are being monitored as part of this biological-control concept, but egg infection and any resulting reduction in WCSB pressure still require field evidence. Last year's very hot summer was associated with severe borer damage in the estate, while nearby growers reported heavy losses despite two or three organophosphorus-insecticide sprays. This is recorded as context for the experiment, not as a controlled comparison of treatments. Natural Predators Spider webs have been observed on almost all coffee plants. This is being treated as a potentially important ecological observation because spiders may capture adult insects before egg laying. The contribution to WCSB suppression remains a field hypothesis requiring continued observation. Why the Borer Has Not Yet Declined Although the lime–neem program and microbial applications may provide indirect ecological benefits, WCSB pressure has not yet clearly declined. One possible explanation is that the major pressure last season was associated with unusually high summer heat, and larvae already established inside stems are not directly reached by a surface spray. The current program is therefore being evaluated mainly as a preventive approach aimed at the adult/egg-laying stage. Pepper Phytophthora / Wilt Approximately 10–12 old affected pepper vines have received repeated microbial-consortium soil drenches and foliar applications. Earlier field notes reported visible regrowth and estimated 50–60% recovery over 5–6 months. Causation has not been isolated. Integrated biological-management philosophy: The estate is not trying to claim that synthetic crop-protection products can never be needed. The research question is whether a combination of healthy soil biology, shade, nutrition, irrigation, biomass recycling, microbial inputs, botanical measures and natural predators can reduce the need for routine synthetic pesticide/fungicide use while maintaining healthy and productive crops. Why Avoid a Chemical-Only Mindset? Some synthetic pesticides, including certain organophosphate insecticides, can pose neurological and occupational risks and may also create risks to non-target organisms. Pesticide fate and residue behaviour differ by active ingredient, product and application conditions. SAARTHA ESTATE therefore focuses on reducing unnecessary reliance on synthetic crop-protection chemicals rather than making blanket claims about every pesticide. “Rather than asking people to simply believe me, I want to document what the field itself shows over time.” 2026 — The Year of Estate Rebuilding After the property was purchased in November 2025, greater attention was given to a previously neglected ~2-acre portion.
